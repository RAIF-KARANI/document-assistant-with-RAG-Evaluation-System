Retrieval Strategies

The core job of retrieval in a RAG system is to take a user's question and
find the small number of document chunks most likely to help answer it.

Semantic search retrieves by comparing embeddings: the question is embedded
into the same vector space as the document chunks, and the chunks whose
vectors are closest to the question's vector are returned. This matches on
meaning rather than exact words, so it can find a relevant chunk even when
the question and the chunk share almost no vocabulary.

Keyword search, also called lexical search, matches on exact words or word
stems, the way a traditional search engine or a simple text search works.
It is fast and precise when the right keywords are known, but it fails
completely when a question is phrased differently from the source text,
even if the meaning is identical.

Hybrid search combines both approaches, typically by running a semantic
search and a keyword search in parallel and merging the results, often with
a weighting scheme that favors one or the other depending on the query.
Hybrid search tends to outperform either method alone, especially for
queries that include specific identifiers, codes, or proper nouns that
embeddings alone sometimes struggle to match precisely.

Reranking is an optional second stage after initial retrieval: a small set
of candidate chunks, larger than the final number needed, is retrieved
quickly with a cheap method like vector similarity, and then a more
expensive model re-scores and reorders those candidates for relevance
before the final top chunks are selected. This two-stage approach balances
speed and quality, since the expensive reranking step only ever looks at a
small candidate set instead of the whole corpus.

The number of chunks retrieved, often called top-k, is a tunable setting
with a real tradeoff. Too few risks missing the chunk that actually
contains the answer. Too many dilutes the language model's context with
irrelevant material, increases cost, and on a small corpus can even mean
retrieving the entire collection regardless of relevance, which defeats the
purpose of retrieval entirely.
